CHAPTER ONE

The problem, and where I fit in it

The Brief
Redesign a working part of Goodreads using research, not opinion.
Scope
Navigation · Home · My Library · Book Profile · Search · Community
Constraints
Desktop · Academic timeline · Three-person team
My focus
Research synthesis, IA strategy, paper prototype evaluation

Goodreads is the largest book-cataloging platform on the internet — and one of the most consistently complained-about. The interface has barely been touched since launch in 2006. Pages are cluttered, search is shallow, the “Updates” feed reads like a stranger’s timeline, and basic acts of reading life — like marking a book DNF, or hiding a guilty-pleasure shelf — are either buried or impossible. The site has 150 million members. They put up with it because nothing else has a database this deep, and because their friends are already there.

Our team set out to redesign Goodreads as a desktop platform: not a paint-job, but a research-driven rebuild of the information architecture and the screens it organizes. We worked through the full UX cycle — interviews, affinity mapping, card sort, tree test, paper prototype, evaluation, high-fidelity design — and our deliverable evolved each round in response to what users actually did, not what we assumed they wanted.

Within the team I leaned into the parts of UX I care about most: research synthesis, information architecture, and turning study findings into specific design decisions. I conducted three of our seven user interviews and led the affinity diagramming; I co-designed and moderated the card sort and tree test, then built the similarity-matrix analysis that anchored our proposed IA. I planned and ran the low-fidelity usability evaluation, and contributed the Homepage and User Input redesigns to the final prototype.

The hardest part wasn’t collecting data — it was making the data argue with us. Card-sort participants were unanimous that a discovery group existed (7/7) but couldn’t agree on what belonged there or what to call it. Tree-test directness scores cratered on tasks where success rates were high — users found the right answer, but only after backtracking through three wrong labels. The work was learning to listen to that gap.

CHAPTER TWO

The approach — five phases

Each phase fed the next. We didn’t collect findings and tuck them in a folder; we used every study’s output to design the next study’s input. The card sort gave us the labels we tested in the tree test; the tree test gave us the IA we wireframed in paper; the paper prototype gave us the priorities we built in Figma.

PHASE 01

Qualitative

Seven user interviews surfaced pain points and reading mental models. Findings were clustered by affinity into five themes.

PHASE 02

Forty-two content items, seven moderated open sorts. We built a similarity matrix to surface natural groupings.

PHASE 03

Tree Test

Nine tasks against our proposed IA, six participants. Success and directness scores told us what worked and what only sort of did.

PHASE 04

Paper Prototype

Eight screens and nine micro-interactions tested in paper with three readers. Surface-level design problems surfaced fast.

PHASE 05

Final Prototype

Interactive Figma. Every component traceable back to a study quote or score. The redesigned Goodreads in motion.

CHAPTER THREE

The methods, in detail

Click through each method to see what we did, what we learned, and the specific decisions it produced. The patterns that repeat — label confusion, the Search/Discovery tension, the privacy split between personal and public content — show up again and again. They didn’t come from one study; they were the only things every study agreed on.

METHOD 01 / QUALITATIVE

Seven interviews, five themes, one affinity board

What we did

I conducted three of our seven user interviews — including an unplanned third session I added when Participant V offered to introduce me to a power user who turned out to be one of our richest data sources. I let participants talk through their actual reading workflows instead of rigidly following our discovery → tracking → reviews script, because most people answered our review questions while still on discovery. We synthesized in FigJam, producing an affinity board organized around Ratings & Reviews, Book Discovery, Book Tracking, Advanced Search, and Community.

7Participants
5Affinity themes
10Sub-clusters
3Interviews I led

What we learned

  • i.
    The 5-star scale is too blunt.

    Multiple participants wanted half-stars or multi-dimensional ratings (plot, pacing, characters). Five-star inflation has made aggregate ratings useless to them.

  • ii.
    People don’t trust algorithmic recs.

    They trust BookTok, BookTube, Reddit, and friends — in that order. The platform’s own recommendations were called “stereotyped” and “random.”

  • iii.
    There’s no Did-Not-Finish shelf.

    Abandoned books pile up under Currently Reading. Deleting them means forgetting you ever tried them.

  • iv.
    Privacy is the missing axis.

    Readers wanted to hide guilty-pleasure shelves and personal notes without taking them off the account entirely.

  • v.
    Community means “in person,” not “forum.”

    Participants wanted local meetups and book clubs, not discussion boards.

They don’t have a do-not-finished section. I feel like it should really be on a shelf. Participant 5 (P5)

The interviews gave us the “what’s missing” list — DNF, privacy, nuanced ratings, local community — and the five themes shaped every later study. Specifically, “Did Not Finish,” “Personal Notes,” and “Privacy Settings” entered the card sort as new labels we hadn’t had to invent — participants had already named them for us.

METHOD 02 / CARD SORT

Forty-two cards, seven mental models

What we did

We ran a moderated open card sort with 42 content items — a mix of existing Goodreads labels, items users had requested in interviews (DNF, Personal Notes, Privacy Settings), and existing features we’d renamed based on interview feedback. Open format meant participants invented their own group names, which is how we surfaced label preferences directly. I built the similarity matrix and synthesized 50 raw groups into 10 unique categories.

42Cards sorted
7Participants
50Raw groups
10Unique categories

What we learned

  • i.
    76–100% agreement on the reading-status core.

    Want to Read, Currently Reading, DNF, and Favorites clustered tightly. Personal Notes, Reading Goals, and Reading Stats formed a clear secondary tier (51–75%) — a natural “activities” sub-group.

  • ii.
    Discovery is universal but unnamed.

    7/7 participants created a discovery group; no one agreed on what should be in it or what to call it. “Browse,” “Explore,” “Discover,” and “Search” were used interchangeably.

  • iii.
    Labels broke before structure did.

    “Custom Shelves” was mistaken for sponsored content; “Review Drafts” was assumed to mean social-post drafts; “Groups” could mean books or people. The underlying concepts scored high agreement — only the labels failed.

  • iv.
    Book Profile is its own thing.

    5/7 participants created a Book Profile group. “Other Books by Author” scored 0–25% with My Library, but always paired with Book Details — strong evidence it belongs on the book page, not in the library.

One is like browsing and one is googling or like searching. Participant 6 — trying to reconcile Search and Discovery

The similarity matrix gave us our proposed IA almost for free: the tight clusters became sections (My Library, Community, Book Profile), the unanimous-but-unnamed cluster became a deliberate distinction (Search and Discovery, separated), and the confusing labels got renamed (“Custom Shelves” → “Custom Tags,” “Review Drafts” → “Unfinished Reviews,” “Groups” → “Book Clubs”).

METHOD 03 / TREE TEST

Nine tasks, and a directness gap we didn’t expect

What we did

We tested the proposed IA from the card sort with six participants doing nine tasks. Each task was phrased as a real user goal (“You recently moved to a new city and want to find book-related meetups”) rather than as a navigation instruction. Moderated, text-only — no visual design, just labels — so any failure pointed at the IA itself.

9Tasks
6Participants
7/9High-success tasks
7/9Low-directness tasks

What we learned

  • i.
    The structure worked. The labels still didn’t.

    Seven of nine tasks hit high success rates — users eventually got to the right place. But seven of nine also had directness scores under 70%, meaning they backtracked through wrong labels first. This is the signature pattern of a labeling problem, not a structural one.

  • ii.
    “Community” was too narrow.

    Participants expected Friends & Following inside Community — but we’d nested it under Home/Landing as the Activity Feed. Multiple participants pulled toward Community first.

  • iii.
    Author was a missing primary.

    4 of 6 participants expected dedicated author-based search and discovery. It existed in our IA only as a book-page feature.

  • iv.
    Account labels collapsed in users’ minds.

    “Preferences,” “Privacy,” and “Settings” were perceived as overlapping. Several participants couldn’t articulate the difference.

Since this is very specifically about my friends, I would click on Community. Participant 2 — finding the Activity Feed

The tree test told us exactly which decisions to revisit in the final IA: add Friends & Following under Community, split Author Search out as its own filter, consolidate Preferences/Privacy/Settings into Profile and Settings, and treat “Activities” as cross-referenced rather than nested-only. Every Final IA change traced back to a specific task’s directness score or a specific participant quote.

METHOD 04 / COMPETITIVE REVIEW

What other platforms already do better

What we did

A targeted competitive scan of platforms readers actually mentioned in interviews — TikTok, Amazon, StoryGraph, AO3, Apple Books — focused not on broad market comparison but on three specific pattern questions: how do other platforms handle reviews at scale? How do they separate personal vs. public content? How do they help users discover by mood, not just genre?

What we learned

  • i.
    Amazon AI-summarizes reviews.

    Keyword extraction lets readers click into the reviews that mentioned “pacing” or “ending” without scrolling through hundreds. Adopted in our Book Profile as Keyword Search.

  • ii.
    TikTok splits the feed into tabs.

    “Following” vs. “For You” cleanly separates social from algorithmic. Influenced our Homepage decision to make Friends Activity a dedicated quadrant.

  • iii.
    AO3 uses combinable tags.

    Multi-tag filtering (“romantic AND dragons”) is a single feature users specifically named as making AO3 superior. Influenced our Custom Tags + Mood Grid in Advanced Search.

  • iv.
    Apple Books prompts at the right moment.

    Asks for a review when the book ends. Goodreads requires you to remember to come back. P6 said this was the single reason she rated more books on Apple Books than Goodreads.

The competitive review wasn’t about copying — it was about finding precedent for things participants told us they wanted. When P5 asked for “romantic AND dragons” filtering, we could point to AO3’s tag system as proof it works at scale. That made the design argument easier to make and harder to dismiss.

METHOD 05 / USABILITY EVALUATION

Paper, scissors, think-aloud

What we did

Three moderated paper-prototype sessions, each led by a different team member, structured around two core task scenarios — Review & Privacy and Search & Exploration — followed by an exploratory walkthrough of screens that didn’t fit cleanly into either task. I built the protocol and ran one of the sessions. The exploratory walkthrough was a methodological choice: secondary screens (popups, alternate views, micro-interactions) are hard to surface through pure task flows, so we built a moderator-led pass that let us prompt reactions to specific elements.

8Screens
9Micro-interactions
3Participants
2+1Tasks + walkthrough

What we learned

  • i.
    The architecture held.

    Participants went straight to the right sections for both tasks. The breakdowns weren’t in where things lived — they were in labels, affordances, and privacy wording.

  • ii.
    Icon-only affordances failed.

    Eye, X, and heart icons on the user-input screen carried real meaning (hide from profile, remove from library, favorite). Only one participant guessed correctly without prompting. We added text labels.

  • iii.
    “Anonymize” was ambiguous.

    Two of three participants couldn’t tell whether it hid the book from the profile or hid their identity on the review. Renamed to “Post Review Anonymously” — the wording itself fixed the problem.

  • iv.
    Discovery was the search shortcut.

    2 of 3 participants went to Discovery first for the Search task, then bounced back. We added a second Advanced Search entry point in the Discovery menu — meeting users where they actually went, instead of insisting on the path we’d intended.

Will the anonymize thing make it so it won’t show up on your profile or will it just make it so that your comment does not have your information? Participant — testing privacy controls

The lo-fi evaluation moved us from “does the IA work” (it did) to “does the design read” (not yet). Every finding became a specific change in the final prototype: action icons got text labels, “Additional Ratings” became “Rate by Criteria,” Discovery got a second Advanced Search entry point, and the Quick Progress popup gained a date field. The cleanest lesson from this round: structural problems and surface problems require different fixes, and not knowing which is which costs you a sprint.

01The Homepage

My contribution: final design lead

The Homepage is the first impression and, on the current Goodreads, the most disappointing one. It welcomes you not with your reading life but with a stranger’s timeline of activity, an ad, and a banner. We rebuilt it as a personal dashboard — and the final layout is the screen I spent the most time on personally.

01
Current StateWhat our research surfaced

A landing page that doesn’t support the things its users land for.

Participants opened Goodreads to track reading progress, see what friends were reading, or pick up where they’d left off. The current Homepage answers none of those goals directly.

  • i.
    “Updates” is ambiguous.

    Users couldn’t tell whether it showed friends’ activity, system-generated suggestions, or trending content. The vague label collapsed three distinct content types into one feed.

  • ii.
    Reading progress lacks prominence.

    The reading summary is buried in a side widget with no visual hierarchy. Users said they wanted a motivational nudge right on the homepage.“I would assume reading habits and reading pace would be either in home or landing page.” — P1

  • iii.
    Recommendations feel corporate, not curated.

    Algorithmic suggestions sit next to ads with no visual separation. Users called the experience “Amazon-feeling” and skipped the section.“It also feels very Amazon… there’s not much of a community aspect.” — P2

  • iv.
    No quick access to currently-reading.

    To update progress, users have to leave the homepage entirely — when the moment they want to log is right there, the second they open the site.

02
Team Lo-FiThe paper prototype

Four quadrants. Four reading-life goals. One page.

As a team we mapped the four jobs users came to the homepage for — track, motivate, browse social, discover — onto a four-quadrant layout. We tested the layout in paper, watching participants react to each block in turn.

03
Final PrototypeMy redesign in Figma

A personal dashboard. The screen I owned.

For the final prototype I took the Homepage lead and translated the lo-fi quadrant layout into a high-fidelity working screen. Most of the additions came directly from lo-fi-eval and tree-test quotes I tagged as “unblocked by paper, blocked by visual.”

Moves from lo-fi to final
  • Switch-book dropdown — P6 mentioned reading three formats simultaneously; the dropdown lets users toggle their active read.
  • Date field in Update Progress popup — added after the lo-fi exploratory walkthrough ("I would have a little date thing so I could update what date I made progress at").
  • Friends Activity instead of Updates — only people the user follows, only specific activity types.
  • Fallback state — "you aren’t currently reading anything" prompts users into their next read instead of showing a blank block.

02My Library

Team contribution

My Library is where the card sort hit hardest: 76–100% of participants grouped every reading-status card together. The current Goodreads scatters those same cards across three pages. The redesign consolidates them — and adds the shelves users literally named in interviews.

01
Current StateWhat our research surfaced

A page named “My Books” that misses the books people actually have.

The current My Books page has three default shelves (Want to Read, Currently Reading, Read), no privacy controls, no DNF option, and a tag system most users don’t know exists.

  • i.
    Missing shelves.

    No Did Not Finish, no Top Reads, no Private Reads — every one of which surfaced as a direct request in interviews.“They don’t have a do not finished section. I feel it should really be on a shelf.” — P5

  • ii.
    No tag association.

    Adding a tag to a book silently creates a sub-shelf, but users don’t know that’s what happened.“I’m like, why is it there? I don’t know what that means for my account.” — P6

  • iii.
    No privacy CTA.

    Users wanted to hide guilty-pleasure books from their public profile without deleting them. There’s no way to do this on the current site.“I wonder if you can make your reviews private.” — P1

  • iv.
    No visual distinction between actions and metadata.

    "Edit" and "View" CTAs look identical to book information, leading users to click past the controls they were looking for.

  • v.
    Consolidated menu options are inconsistently used.

    Many top-level CTA buttons rarely get touched; others are buried.

02
Team Lo-FiThe paper prototype

Two views, one structure: Cover and Table.

We tested the assumption that different readers think about their library differently. Cover-first browsers (visual) and table-first power readers (data-dense) got two interchangeable views built on the same data. The lo-fi eval confirmed both were needed.

03
Final PrototypeThe team’s redesign

The home base for your reading life.

The final design folds in lo-fi-eval fixes: text labels on action icons, decimal ratings, custom-tag clarifications, and explicit privacy controls. Most of the changes were surface-level — the structure tested by paper held.

Moves from lo-fi to final
  • Action-icon labels — only 1 of 3 paper-prototype participants recognized the eye icon. We added labels (Hide from Profile, Remove from Library, Add to Top Reads).
  • Rating granularity — decimal ratings replaced fixed 5-star, after multiple interview participants wanted half-stars.
  • Custom Tags clarifications — explicit visual distinction between default shelves and user-created tags.
  • New shelf categories — Did Not Finish, My Top Reads, Private Reads all surfaced from user quotes.

03User Input

My contribution: final design lead

User Input is the second page I owned in the final prototype. It’s the overlay readers see when they want to rate, review, or log a book — and it’s where the lo-fi evaluation surfaced more usability issues than any other screen. Every label on this page got rewritten after paper testing.

01
Current StateWhat our research surfaced

One flat form trying to do three different jobs.

Reviewing a book, tracking what you read, and rating it for criteria are three distinct mental modes. The current Goodreads collapses them into a single form with no privacy logic.

  • i.
    Rating granularity.

    Five stars cannot capture nuance. Users wanted half-stars or multi-dimensional ratings.“Some books I felt were closer to a five than a four — that’s not really reflected in just the basic five star system.” — P6

  • ii.
    Multiple review formats.

    Users wanted optional structured fields (plot, characters, content warnings) alongside free-text reviews. The current single-field text input forces everyone into the same shape.

  • iii.
    No version history.

    Re-reading a book years later changes how you feel about it — users wanted to update reviews without losing the original.“Sometimes I’ll update previous reviews… a few years after and be like that actually had more of an effect on me.” — P1

  • iv.
    Normalize text entry.

    The current review field uses markdown syntax — uncommon knowledge for most readers — and breaks formatting if used wrong.

  • v.
    Consolidated progress tracking and review entry.

    Logging progress and writing a review are different intents but the same form. Date fields get skipped because they’re not prompted.

02
Team Lo-FiThe paper prototype

Split the modes. Tabs, not fields.

The lo-fi prototype split user input into two tabs on the same surface: Ratings & Review (public) and Personal Tracking (private). The lo-fi evaluation revealed which labels survived and which didn’t.

03
Final PrototypeMy redesign in Figma

Where evaluation findings landed in pixels.

The User Input screen is the clearest illustration of what the lo-fi evaluation does: every confused term in paper became a renamed component in Figma. I led this page’s final design and turned each finding into a specific edit.

Moves from lo-fi to final
  • “Additional Ratings” → “Rate by Criteria” — paper-prototype users thought “Additional Ratings” meant other people’s ratings. Renamed and added a tooltip info icon.
  • “Anonymize” → “Post Review Anonymously” — 2 of 3 lo-fi participants couldn’t tell what would be hidden. The new wording fixed the confusion before any redesign was needed.
  • Quick-action icons get text labels — Heart → Add to Top Reads, Eye → Hide from Profile, X → Remove from Library.
  • Version history on reviews — added after P1’s quote about updating old reviews without losing the original.

04Advanced Search

Team contribution

Search is where the card sort fractured: every participant said discovery existed, no one said where. Our resolution was to make Search and Discovery distinct entry points with overlapping content — and to treat the Advanced Search page as a discovery surface, not a query field.

01
Current StateWhat our research surfaced

A single text field doing the work of ten filters.

The current Goodreads search is one open input. There’s no advanced filtering, no mood-based discovery, no author-first path, and no way to combine criteria.

  • i.
    Limited advanced search.

    Users couldn’t combine genre + mood + setting. The granular searches readers wanted were impossible.“I want something that — contemporary romance that’s set outside of the US. And then for whatever reason, I just can’t find it.” — P6

  • ii.
    Search lacks guidance.

    The bar gives no hint about what users can search for or how to refine. There’s no filter affordance until after a query is run.

  • iii.
    Unclear search categories.

    Books / People / Listopia tabs are positioned below the query, where users overlook them. Most users default to Books even when they want something else.

  • iv.
    Author-based search is missing.

    4 of 6 tree-test participants expected to search by author as a first-class action. The current site treats it as a side-effect of book search.

  • v.
    Unique search categories aren’t supported.

    Mood, vibe, content-warning — none of these exist as filters even though they came up repeatedly in interviews.

02
Team Lo-FiThe paper prototype

Search as a discovery surface, not a question.

The paper version reframed the page around discovery: a search bar at top, filter tabs (Books, Authors, Series, etc.), a left sidebar for genre & rating, a mood grid for vibe-based discovery, and a Reader’s Trust row of curated entries.

03
Final PrototypeThe team’s redesign

Multiple paths to one book.

The final Advanced Search supports five simultaneous discovery paths: text search, filter tabs, genre/rating checkboxes, author search, and mood discovery. Every applied filter becomes a dismissible tag.

Moves from lo-fi to final
  • Author search promoted to sidebar — its own persistent field, always visible regardless of other filters.
  • Active filter tags as chips — every filter visible at the top of the results page, individually dismissible.
  • By-rating moved from sort to filter — quality threshold before seeing results, not a re-sort after.
  • Results show synopsis and Add-to-Library — one-click action without leaving the results page.
  • Discovery menu in nav gets a second entry point to Advanced Search, after 2 of 3 lo-fi participants went there first.

05Book Profile

Team contribution

Book Profile is the page readers spend the most time on, and the page our card sort identified as its own structural entity (5 of 7 participants). The current Goodreads book page is a long undifferentiated scroll. Our redesign breaks it into four scannable zones.

01
Current StateWhat our research surfaced

One long scroll with no visual hierarchy.

The current Book Profile leads with an ad, mixes critical details with peripheral metadata, and buries the reviews most readers actually open the page for.

  • i.
    Information overload.

    All the information on this page is visible at once in long scroll format, with little visual distinction between sections.

  • ii.
    Primary actions lack visibility.

    "Currently reading" and rating CTAs have no visual prominence, leading to low engagement.

  • iii.
    Ad sponsors interrupt the flow.

    Amazon ads break up the visual hierarchy between important sections, making the ad look like the main content.“I did not like that experience.” — P4

  • iv.
    Reviews lack credibility signals.

    Users can’t tell which reviewers are credentialed and which are casual. No verified-reader badge, no source.“Like professional book reviewers, if there was a way to distinguish like a professional opinion.” — P5

  • v.
    Language filters as exclusion.

    Setting a language preference hides all reviews in other languages — even ones a translation would unlock.“The summary wasn’t in a language that I knew.” — P4

  • vi.
    Editions section is over-prominent.

    Editions of the same book take more visual real estate than community discussions or author work.

02
Team Lo-FiThe paper prototype

Four bordered zones. One scannable page.

The lo-fi prototype used Gestalt grouping (proximity, common region) to split the page into Book Details, Author Section, Community Reviews, and Recommendations — with ads moved to a dedicated side column.

03
Final PrototypeThe team’s redesign

From long scroll to scannable surface.

The final Book Profile keeps the four-zone structure but adds the interaction details users named in interviews — content warnings, keyword review search, and translation toggles.

Moves from lo-fi to final
  • Content warnings surfaced in Book Details — directly requested in interviews (P6).
  • Keyword Search across reviews — adapted from Amazon’s AI review summarization (competitive review).
  • Translate per review — replaces the language-filter exclusion with an opt-in translation button.
  • User credibility badges — verified reviewers visually distinguished from casual users.
  • Friends & Following review filter — surfaces reviews from people the user trusts.
  • Ads moved to side column — out of the content flow, visible without being interruptive.

06Community

Future scope

Community received its fair share of research insight — but didn’t make it into our final prototype. The lo-fi evaluation, tree test, and interviews all surfaced strong direction here, and the section is fully resolved in the Final IA. The visual design is what’s missing. It’s the most-mature unbuilt page in the project.

WHAT THE RESEARCH ALREADY KNOWS

The Community we didn’t get to design.

The current Community tab is one of the most under-utilized parts of Goodreads. Users want a centralized community hub to discover and follow readers, find local events, stay updated, and join discussions. They also prefer book clubs that offer flexible, self-paced reading, and they want an easy way to give away unfinished books. None of this was a guess — it came directly from interviews, the card sort, and tree-test directness scores.

Three insights are concrete enough to design from on day one:

Friends & Following ≠ Activity Feed

Tree-test participants expected social-graph navigation to live under Community, not Home. The Final IA adds a dedicated Friends & Following sub-section.

Local + digital book clubs

Users wanted hybrid book clubs — both online and IRL. The Local Networks sub-section in the Final IA is where this lives, but the UI to support it hasn’t been designed yet.

People Leaderboards & events

Surfaced through card-sort participant language. Local events near you, top community readers, book club picks — all already in the IA, all waiting on visuals.

CHAPTER SEVEN

The conclusion

What we’ve built so far is a research-anchored redesign of Goodreads’ desktop platform — a final information architecture, five reworked core screens, and an in-progress Figma prototype that walks through the central reading-life flows. The project was received across three reviews and a final critique as one of the more research-rigorous in the cohort: reviewers specifically noted that every design decision had a study source attached to it. It’s also, intentionally, unfinished. Several pages — Community most notably — are at the point where the research is solved but the visuals aren’t.

The personal lesson is one I’ll carry forward. The data didn’t always agree with our hypotheses, and the times it didn’t were the times the project got better. The card sort told us discovery was universal and unnameable — that became the “Search vs. Discovery” split. The tree test told us our IA worked but our labels didn’t — that became four specific renames. The paper prototype told us the architecture held but the affordances failed — that became labeled icons and renamed checkboxes. Every time we treated a finding as a challenge instead of a confirmation, the design got sharper.

If I were continuing the project, the next visual would be Community — the page where the research is loudest and the design is most absent. After that, I’d push the existing screens out of template territory into fully interactive states, then run an unmoderated round against the higher-fidelity build. Moderated sessions are great for understanding why, but they protect users from the worst friction. And then the obvious step: mobile, which we deliberately scoped out but is where most readers actually live.

If I had another semester

  1. Design the Community page — every other page got a visual treatment; this one didn’t, despite having strong research direction.
  2. Move from template to interactive — finish wiring up the screens that exist; bring secondary states into the prototype.
  3. Unmoderated usability study — to see where users get stuck without a moderator’s help.
  4. Mobile redesign — desktop was scope; mobile is where the use lives.
  5. Accessibility audit — color contrast, screen reader flows, keyboard nav.
  6. Onboarding flow — P6 mentioned Goodreads felt “very blank” for new users.
